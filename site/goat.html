Greats of All Time - Explore the greatest authors and their masterpieces

Crime and Punishment

1866

His most famous work. A psychological thriller about a poor student who commits murder and struggles with guilt, exploring themes of redemption and morality. Widely considered one of the greatest novels ever written.

The Idiot

1869

Explores the clash between goodness and innocence in a corrupt society. Features Prince Myshkin, an epileptic idealist navigating a world of greed and deception.

Demons

1872

A political novel that portrays revolutionary nihilism and its destructive consequences. Set in a provincial Russian town, it examines ideology, betrayal, and the search for meaning.

The Brothers Karamazov

1879

His masterpiece. An epic family saga exploring faith, doubt, suffering, and morality through the lives of four brothers. Features the famous "Grand Inquisitor" chapter, one of literature's greatest philosophical passages.

Notes from Underground

1864

A philosophical novella exploring existential themes and proto-existentialist ideas. A lonely, bitter man reflects on his life and challenges rationalist philosophy. Short but profound.

The Gambler

1866

A novella about addiction and obsession. Written quickly to meet a deadline, it explores the psychology of gambling addiction with intense psychological realism.

Poor Folk

1846

His debut novel, written in epistolary form (letters between two characters). A touching story of poverty and love, introducing themes that would dominate his later works.

The Eternal Husband

1870

A novella exploring jealousy, love, and complex relationships. A masterclass in psychological character study, examining human nature in a relatively short work.

White Nights

1848

A romantic novella about a lonely young man's chance encounter with a mysterious woman in St. Petersburg. Poetic and melancholic, exploring loneliness and dreams.

Recommended Reading Path

Step 1: Start Small - Notes from Underground 1864

Begin with this short philosophical novella. At around 100 pages, it introduces Dostoevsky's major themes without overwhelming commitment. It's existential, dark, and sets up his philosophical preoccupations perfectly.

Why: Proves Dostoevsky's genius in a shorter format; proto-existentialist ideas are mind-expanding.

Step 2: Crime and Punishment 1866

Read this as your first full novel. Despite its dark subject matter (murder and guilt), it's more accessible than his later epics. The psychological intensity is gripping from page one.

Why: His most famous work; a psychological thriller that reads fast; explores redemption and morality beautifully.

Step 3: The Idiot 1869

Now tackle this masterpiece about goodness in a corrupt world. It's longer than Crime and Punishment, but the protagonist (Prince Myshkin) is one of literature's greatest characters.

Why: Explores idealism vs. reality; features a Christ-like character navigating human cruelty; philosophically profound.

Step 4: The Brothers Karamazov 1879

Save this for last. It's his longest and most ambitious work–”a family saga that explores faith, doubt, suffering, and love. The "Grand Inquisitor" chapter alone is worth the entire reading experience.

Why: His absolute masterpiece; synthesizes all his themes; rewards careful reading; life-changing for many readers.

Optional: Demons 1872

If you want more after the above, explore this political novel. It's his most satirical work and offers sharp commentary on revolutionary nihilism. Dense but brilliant.

Why: Political depth; darkly comic; portrays the seductive danger of ideology.

Fascinating Trivia

The Mock Execution (1849): Dostoevsky was arrested for reading banned literature and sentenced to death. He faced the firing squad, but at the last moment, his sentence was commuted to hard labor in Siberia. This traumatic event profoundly shaped his philosophical and spiritual worldview and appears in his works.
Epilepsy: Dostoevsky suffered from severe epilepsy. He experienced mystical-seeming auras before seizures, which influenced his spiritual philosophy. The main character in The Idiot (Prince Myshkin) is also epileptic.
Crime and Punishment was serialized: Published month-by-month in a literary journal, forcing readers to wait anxiously for each installment–”much like modern TV series.
Gambling Addiction: Dostoevsky struggled with severe gambling addiction, which bankrupted him multiple times. He wrote The Gambler while heavily in debt, partly to make money.
Stenographer-turned-wife: Dostoevsky married his stenographer, Anna Grigoryevna Snitkina, who was 25 years younger. She helped organize his life and archives, proving invaluable to his later works.
The Brothers Karamazov was his last novel: Published in 1879, the year before his death. It's considered his spiritual and artistic culmination.

War and Peace

1869

His masterpiece and one of the longest novels ever written. An epic historical novel following Russian society during the Napoleonic Wars. Explores war, peace, love, destiny, and the forces that shape history. A pinnacle of world literature.

Anna Karenina

1877

An epic romance exploring love, society, family, and the search for meaning. Opens with the famous line: "All happy families are alike; each unhappy family is unhappy in its own way." Features multiple interwoven storylines and profound philosophical depth.

The Cossacks

1863

A shorter novel that serves as an excellent introduction to Tolstoy's style. A young aristocrat joins a Cossack community and experiences a spiritual awakening. More accessible than his epics while showcasing his genius.

Resurrection

1899

Written after Tolstoy's spiritual conversion, this novel explores redemption and social justice. A nobleman's chance encounter with a woman from his past triggers a spiritual awakening and critique of Russian society and law.

The Kreutzer Sonata

1891

A novella exploring marriage, passion, jealousy, and obsession. A man recounts his marriage's emotional and physical trajectory on a train. Controversial and psychologically intense, written after Tolstoy's spiritual crisis.

Master and Man

1895

A novella about a wealthy merchant and his servant caught in a snowstorm. Explores greed, sacrifice, and spiritual transformation. Shows how a life-threatening situation can strip away materialism and reveal human kindness.

Family Happiness

1859

An early novella told from a woman's perspective, exploring marriage and romantic love. A young woman marries an older man and discovers that married happiness differs from romantic idealization. Precursor to his later marital themes.

Ivan the Fool

1885

A fairy tale-like novella with a simple peasant as the wise protagonist. Critiques materialism and ambition while celebrating simplicity and spiritual wisdom. Uniquely allegorical for Tolstoy.

Hadji Murat

1912

Published posthumously, this novella is set during Russian conflicts in the Caucasus. Explores honor, freedom, and the futility of war through the story of a legendary warrior. A masterclass in compressed storytelling.

Recommended Reading Path

Step 1: The Cossacks 1863

Start with this shorter novel (around 250 pages). It introduces Tolstoy's style, his gift for character development, and his philosophical depth without the overwhelming length of his epics. A young aristocrat discovers spiritual awakening among the Cossacks.

Why: Perfect introduction; shows his genius in a more digestible length; lyrical and philosophical.

Step 2: Anna Karenina 1877

Read this epic before War and Peace. While still lengthy, it's more character-focused and emotionally immediate. The opening line is one of literature's greatest. Multiple storylines explore love, family, society, and the search for meaning.

Why: More intimate than War and Peace; explores universal human experiences; beautifully written.

Step 3: War and Peace 1869

Tackle his longest masterpiece last. Over 1,000 pages, it's an ambitious historical epic following Russian society during the Napoleonic Wars. Combines intimate character stories with historical philosophy. Many readers consider it life-changing.

Why: His absolute masterpiece; rewards patient reading; explores war, peace, love, history, and destiny.

Step 4: Resurrection 1899

After the epics, explore his final major novel, written after his spiritual conversion. It's more focused and critiques Russian society and law through the spiritual journey of a nobleman. Shows his philosophical evolution.

Why: Synthesizes his later spiritual beliefs; more accessible than his epics; powerful redemption narrative.

Optional: Shorter Works

Once you've read the major novels, explore his novellas: The Kreutzer Sonata (marriage and obsession), Master and Man (greed vs. kindness), or Ivan the Fool (simplicity as wisdom). These show different facets of his genius.

Why: More focused explorations of specific themes; show his versatility; reveal his spiritual evolution.

Fascinating Trivia

War and Peace length: Depending on the edition, War and Peace is between 1,000-1,400+ pages. It's one of the longest novels ever written. Some editions split it into multiple volumes.
Military service: Tolstoy served as an officer in the Russian army and fought in the Caucasus, which directly informed his writing of War and Peace and his Caucasus stories.
Spiritual conversion: In 1882, Tolstoy experienced a profound spiritual crisis and converted to a radical Christian philosophy. His later works (Resurrection, The Kreutzer Sonata, Ivan the Fool) reflect this transformation toward asceticism and social criticism.
Anna Karenina's opening: "All happy families are alike; each unhappy family is unhappy in its own way." Voted one of the greatest opening lines in literature. Sets the novel's themes perfectly.
Writing process: Tolstoy was a perfectionist who revised obsessively. War and Peace took him 5 years to write (1864-1869), and he rewrote passages countless times.
Fled his estate: Near the end of his life, Tolstoy abandoned his family and noble estate, seeking to live according to his radical Christian beliefs. He died at a railway station while running away.
Historical accuracy: Both War and Peace and Anna Karenina feature real historical figures and events woven into fictional narratives. Tolstoy's blend of history and fiction was revolutionary.
